Porting legacy complexity to SaaS — without losing what enterprises depend on.

Migrating CSG's catalog-management and customer-care modules off an aging Silverlight system into Ascendon — both ported to 100% functional parity — while redesigning the nested, workaround-laden workflows that made the legacy experience slow and error-prone.

Product · Ascendon (CSG) Users · CSRs & catalog admins Role · UX/UI Designer Focus · Legacy → SaaS parity & usability

The problem

A decade-old Silverlight platform had to become a modern SaaS product — at 100% feature parity — while the legacy UX was punishing: users lost in nested catalog workflows, and a call center run on hand-made cheat-sheets.

What I designed

The Offers catalog workflow and the Customer Care dashboard — rebuilt for the web with orientation, breadcrumbs, and a shared source of truth, matching every legacy capability.

The bet

Parity didn't mean re-skinning the old flows. I treated the users' own workarounds — cheat-sheets, a taped-up error-decoder — as the brief for what to actually fix.

01 Overview

Lifting years of interdependent functionality off legacy — intact.

Ascendon is CSG's cloud subscription, billing, and monetization platform. Building it as a modern SaaS product meant lifting years of deeply interdependent functionality out of a legacy Silverlight system without losing the capabilities enterprise customers depended on every day.

I joined as a UX Designer on a team of six, each owning different modules, and worked across the two hardest-to-port areas of the product:

  • Studio (catalog management) — where admins build, structure, and price the products customers subscribe to.
  • Customer Care — where customer-service reps (CSRs) resolve billing questions and manage accounts.

The result: both modules were fully ported from legacy to Ascendon — 100% of the functionality migrated — and the redesigns simplified the workflows that had made the legacy experience error-prone.

04 Part 1 — Studio: porting the catalog

"Wizard" didn't mean more steps. It meant orientation.

Catalog management is where the business models its products — and in the legacy system, it was punishing. Research with the client-services team surfaced two clear themes:

  • Users got lost in the nested, dependent workflows required to create a product.
  • They asked for a "wizard-like" guided flow. But most of these tasks were already step-by-step in the legacy system — so the real need wasn't more steps. It was orientation: knowing where you are, what's required, and how to get back.

That reframe — "wizard" meant guidance, not linearity — is what shaped my approach to Offers.

Designing the Offers workflow

Working from our shared ideation sessions, I created block-frames for the Offers entity focused on making the structure legible: clear hierarchy, a persistent sense of place, and a breadcrumb path so users could drill into pricing detail and find their way back without losing context. I presented the direction in our weekly product-and-engineering touchpoint, folded in feedback, and moved to high fidelity.

05 Part 2 — Customer Care

Designing for people who'd already built their own tools.

In my second year I moved primarily to Customer Care, and pushed to ground it in real research rather than assumptions. Our UX Lead and I spent two days at a customer site — a day and a half in the customer-care center and a half-day in the retail store where many care-initiated orders were completed.

The retail store — contextual inquiry

The most telling observations were physical: CSRs had built their own toolkit. Printed code sheets pinned to monitors, cheat-sheets tucked around the cubicle, personal shortcuts layered on top of the software. One estimate was that only a small part of a CSR's job is formally trained — the rest is learned on the floor.

The most damning artifact was a printed error-decoder taped to the desk — a hand-maintained key translating the legacy system's cryptic messages (A3C24E-HSE IS BULK BLL SRV, ACCT IS NOT) into plain-language causes and fixes. When people keep a decoder ring for your error messages, the error messages are the problem.

The error-decoder taped to the desk — translating the legacy system's cryptic messages

That raised the questions that drove the redesign: What product deficiencies were painful enough to require a workaround? And do those workarounds actually work — or do they create more work? The visit also let us influence roadmap items beyond our immediate scope — primary navigation and the customer dashboard — grounded in what we'd seen rather than what we'd assumed. Throughout, I worked closely with the solution and development teams so the directions we explored stayed within what existing APIs could support.

The key insight

The top reason a customer calls is to dispute or understand their bill — usually a misunderstanding, not an actual billing error. So the dashboard puts the same invoice the customer is looking at directly in front of the CSR — turning a defensive conversation into a shared walk-through, line by line. It solves an empathy problem, not just a data-access one.

I paired that with a few decisions aimed squarely at the behaviors we'd watched on-site:

  • A secondary persistent navigation so CSRs always know where they are in the system.
  • The top five most frequently asked questions surfaced on the first screen a CSR sees when pulling up an account.
  • Frequently used actions, customer profile, and recent-activity history brought into reach — folding the cheat-sheet behaviors back into the product itself.

07 Reflection

In enterprise tools, the workarounds are the research.

The cheat-sheets taped to a CSR's monitor told us more about what the product was missing than any survey could. The most valuable thing we could do wasn't to design something clever — it was to make the system finally do the job people had been quietly compensating for.
