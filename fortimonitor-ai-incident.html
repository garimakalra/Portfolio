The problem

Detection outpaces comprehension. Engineers drown in alerts and burn time gathering context before they can even begin to solve.

What I designed

An AI Investigator embedded in the incident page that synthesizes a summary, timeline, evidence and next steps — with facts and AI hypotheses visibly separated.

The staff-level bet

Chose calibrated assistance over automation. The AI investigates; the human decides. Trust is the feature, not the risk.

01 The problem

Faster detection created a slower kind of work.

FortiMonitor is a cloud infrastructure and network monitoring platform. It's genuinely good at what it does — it finds anomalies across applications, infrastructure, networks and cloud, and generates a constant stream of metrics and alerts.

That strength is also the trap. The system can flag a problem in seconds; a human still needs minutes or hours to understand it. Between the alert firing and the incident being understood, an engineer has to manually decide which alerts matter, find relationships between unrelated-looking events, dig through logs and dashboards, review historical incidents, loop in teammates, and only then reason about root cause and remediation.

That gap has a cost. It inflates mean-time-to-resolution, breeds alert fatigue, and quietly concentrates all the real knowledge in a few senior engineers who've simply seen it before.

02 Research

I talked to the people living inside the alert stream.

I ran two interviews from different vantage points: a Site Reliability Engineer who investigates incidents daily, and a Customer Success Manager who had onboarded one of FortiMonitor's largest enterprise customers and watched their operations up close. One gave me the texture of the work; the other gave me the scale of it.

Interview 01 · SRE

The engineer in the loop

Investigation is mostly context-gathering — pulling from multiple tools, logs, dashboards and past incidents before any real problem-solving starts. The same investigation steps repeat across incidents, and finding dependencies and root cause quickly is the hard part.

An assistant that correlated the relevant signals, summarized the incident, and suggested next steps would meaningfully cut cognitive load.
Interview 02 · CSM

The view from scale

Large customers live in constant firefighting mode. Alerts get round-robin distributed across a team; people jump on the newest one, and often the issue has already resolved itself by the time it's picked up — so the underlying cause is never fixed.

Noise doesn't just tire the internal team — it spills into subcontracted field crews, making external ticketing harder to manage too.

Three findings that shaped the design

01

Alert fatigue is an operational risk, not just an annoyance

Teams monitoring 1,000+ devices receive huge volumes of low-priority, repetitive alerts. Over time they desensitize — and the real danger is a critical incident lost in the noise.

Design opportunity Instead of treating every alert equally, help answer one question: what requires attention first? — weighing severity, customer impact, affected services and similar past incidents.
02

Investigation is fragmented across too many systems

Before solving anything, engineers assemble context from dashboards, logs, traces, deployment history, config changes, docs and prior tickets — all scattered across different tools.

Design opportunity Auto-assemble the incident: affected systems, timeline, related alerts, recent changes, possible causes and recommended investigation steps — in one place.
03

Root-cause skill lives in a few senior heads

Experienced engineers resolve faster because they carry system dependencies and failure patterns in their heads. Newer engineers stall — and the org becomes dependent on a handful of people.

Design opportunity Make the AI a knowledge surface: similar past incidents, previous resolutions, relevant docs and dependency relationships — available to everyone, not just veterans.
03 Framing the design problem

The real question wasn't "can AI do this?" — it was "how much should it?"

It would have been easy to design an AI that just tells engineers the answer. But this domain punishes overconfidence: these are production systems, the AI reasons under uncertainty, and a wrong "root cause" delivered confidently is worse than no answer at all. An engineer who stops thinking is a liability.

So I reframed the brief around a tension rather than a feature:

How might we help engineers understand and resolve incidents faster — while keeping them firmly in control and appropriately skeptical?

That single reframe drove every subsequent decision: what the AI is allowed to claim, how uncertainty is shown, where automation stops, and how trust is earned rather than assumed. The rest of this case study is really about answering that question well.

04 Prototyping & testing

I tested the concept before I tested the pixels.

I built two prototypes deliberately, and ran them in a deliberate order — low-fidelity first, high-fidelity second — around a single sample Host Unreachable incident. Two participants (an SRE and an engineering manager) walked through both.

Lo-fi · Wizard of Oz

Is the concept even worth building?

Wireframes only; I revealed investigation results manually while participants thought aloud. Stripped of visual polish, the conversation went straight to usefulness and trust — participants proposed new capabilities (topology-aware grouping, historical comparisons, runbook integration, automated countermeasures) rather than critiquing layout.

Hi-fi · Figma Make

Would people actually use it?

A polished, interactive interface. Attention shifted to information hierarchy and interaction — panel size, section order, timeline presentation, confidence indicators, and how clearly AI output was distinguished from raw telemetry. The concept was assumed; adoption details surfaced.

The two fidelities weren't redundant — they answered different questions. The lo-fi validated whether the problem was worth solving before I invested in craft; the hi-fi exposed the presentation and trust details that would decide adoption. That's the whole argument for prototyping at two fidelities, and I got to see it play out cleanly.

05 The design

The AI Investigator, inside the incident page.

Rather than a separate tool to context-switch into, the Investigator lives where engineers already are. It does the time-consuming first pass — correlating signals, building the timeline, gathering evidence, proposing likely causes and next steps — and hands the engineer a working hypothesis instead of a blank page.

↳ AI Summary
The entry point. One synthesized paragraph gives immediate context without disrupting the existing workflow — validated in testing as the right place to start.
↳ Confidence
A visible confidence signal so engineers calibrate how much to trust the result — not a hidden black box.
↳ Timeline
Observed events (solid teal) are things the system measured. Inferred events (dashed violet) are the AI's reconstruction. The border style tells you which is which at a glance.
↳ Evidence
The observed facts, collected for you — the panel that replaces the manual scavenger hunt across tools.
↳ Likely cause
Presented as a hypothesis, dashed and labeled — never as a confirmed root cause. This is the single most important restraint in the design.
↳ Next steps + feedback
Suggested steps the engineer chooses to take, and a one-tap way to teach the system whether it helped.

06 Designing for trust

The most important thing the AI does is admit what it doesn't know.

An AI investigating production incidents is reasoning under uncertainty. The failure mode isn't just being wrong — it's being wrong convincingly, and having an engineer act on a confident hallucination. So I made uncertainty a first-class citizen of the interface. Every piece of information the Investigator shows declares its own epistemic status.

Observed

What the system measured

Directly recorded facts — a ping failure, a packet-loss reading, an incident timestamp. Presented as evidence, in solid teal.

ping → 0/5 replies
packet_loss → 62%
ts → 14:32:04 UTC
Inferred

What the AI believes

Hypotheses the AI reasoned toward — a likely upstream link failure. Presented as a claim to evaluate, dashed in violet, never as settled truth.

hypothesis → upstream link degradation
basis → correlated switch packet loss
status → unconfirmed

This one distinction does a lot of quiet work. It lets a senior engineer skim the observed facts and move on; it gives a junior engineer a defensible reasoning trail to learn from; and it structurally prevents the product from over-claiming. The AI communicates what is known, what it believes, and why — and lets the human decide how much confidence to spend.

07 The strategic decision

08 Why the Investigator won

It does the work that's tedious — and stops before the work that's consequential.

The Advisor was too passive to move the metric that mattered: it left the heavy context-gathering on the engineer. The Operator was too eager: letting AI initiate actions against production systems introduces exactly the over-reliance and blast-radius risk this domain can't afford.

The Investigator sits at the honest middle. It performs the expensive first pass — correlating signals, spotting patterns, summarizing evidence — without making a single consequential change on the engineer's behalf. It makes uncertainty visible and hands over a hypothesis, not a verdict. That's the definition of appropriate reliance: people get the speed of automation while keeping responsibility for the decision.

How control stays with the human

Four commitments hold the line:

Recommendations, not instructions. Next steps are suggestions the engineer chooses to take.
No confirmed root cause. The AI offers a labeled hypothesis, never a declared verdict.
Observed vs. inferred, always visible. Engineers can independently assess the evidence.
Consequential actions require approval. Any future automation stops at the human gate.
Design principle

Let the AI do the investigation work. Let the engineer make the consequential decision.

09 Impact & reflection

What it's designed to change — and what I'd carry forward.

This is a concept project, so I'll be honest about the difference between what was validated and what is projected. Validated in testing: the summary-first entry point, the value of separating observed from inferred, and the demand for evidence and confidence signals. Designed to deliver: lower investigation time and less context-switching by collapsing the manual scavenger hunt into one synthesized view — without the over-reliance risk that fuller automation would introduce.

What this project taught me

01 Fidelity is a research instrument

Running lo-fi before hi-fi wasn't process theater. The Wizard of Oz round protected the concept from premature polish and surfaced capability gaps the pretty prototype would have hidden. I'll keep sequencing fidelity to the question I'm asking.

02 With AI, restraint is the design

The hardest and most valuable decisions were about what the AI should not do — not claim certainty, not act on production, not replace judgment. Designing the guardrails was more consequential than designing the features.

03 Trust is built in the interface, not the model

Observed-vs-inferred, visible confidence, and hypotheses-not-verdicts are all UI decisions. The same underlying AI could feel trustworthy or reckless depending purely on how its uncertainty is expressed.

04 Where I'd take it next

Testers pointed to the roadmap: topology-aware incident grouping, separating historical from currently-related incidents, runbook integration, and forecasting to catch anomalies before they become incidents. Natural next bets, grounded in real asks.
