01 The situation I walked into

A product built for one team, now expected to serve a fabric.

FortiMonitor — formerly Panopta, a Chicago monitoring startup — did one thing well: watch infrastructure, networks, and applications in real time and surface issues before they became outages. But the acquisition changed the job. The product now had to sit inside Fortinet's broader security ecosystem, absorb identity and access concerns, and scale to customers far larger than its original audience.

The navigation hadn't kept up. Features had been added where there was room, not where they belonged. Settings alone had grown to thirteen top-level items — a junk drawer where account, integrations, alerting, and access controls sat side by side with no logic a user could predict. The roadmap wanted to bolt identity management and fabric integration onto that. My first job was to argue against doing exactly that.

02 The call I made

Fix the foundation before bolting on the future.

The pressure was to ship the new capabilities fast. I brought the navigation data to the roadmap review and made the opposite case: adding identity and fabric features to a structure users already couldn't navigate wouldn't extend the product — it would accelerate its collapse. New power would land in a place no one could find.

I proposed a phased sequence instead of a simultaneous build: restructure the core IA first, then layer in identity and access management against the new taxonomy, then integrate the security fabric on a structure that could actually hold it. It meant asking the org to delay visible features for invisible groundwork — the kind of trade that's easy to lose in a roadmap meeting.

The deliverable wasn't a sitemap. It was convincing the room that sequence was the design decision.

03 Reading the evidence

The card sort showed a mental model. I had to decide when to honor it and when to override it.

I ran an open card sort with users across the product's real personas, then a tree test to validate. The affinity data resolved into clear clusters — but clustering isn't a structure. The interesting work was interpretation: users grouped some things by habit and workaround, not by what would serve them once the product grew.

Observed is where users put things today. Designed is where I decided they belong. Reading the gap between them was the actual IA work.

Three interpretation moves defined the restructure. Two honored the data — where the users' model was right and the old nav was fighting it. One overrode it — where the observed grouping encoded a limitation of the old product that the new taxonomy needed to break.

04 The restructure

Thirteen settings became six task-based destinations.

The core move was replacing an object-based dumping ground with destinations named for what people were trying to accomplish. Below is the before and after of the top level.

Before · 13-item settings drawer
  • Account
  • Users
  • Notifications
  • Integrations
  • API Keys
  • Billing
  • Alert Rules
  • Escalations
  • Maintenance
  • Tags
  • Access Control
  • Audit Log
  • Preferences
After · 6 task-based destinations
  • Monitoring
  • Alerting & Escalation
  • Integrations & API
  • Access & Identity
  • Account & Billing
  • Audit & Activity

Five structural decisions carried the redesign:

D1

Named destinations by task, not by object

"Alerting & Escalation" instead of scattering Alert Rules, Escalations, and Maintenance windows across the drawer. The name matches the job.

D2

Carved out Access & Identity as its own home

The override move — the card sort buried access control, but the incoming IAM work needed a first-class destination the old model didn't anticipate.

D3

Collapsed configuration noise into context

Tags, preferences, and per-object settings moved next to the objects they configure, out of the global drawer entirely.

D4

Made auditing a destination, not a footnote

Audit log and activity became a named place — necessary the moment enterprise access and compliance entered scope.

D5

Left deliberate room for the fabric

The taxonomy was designed so fabric integration would slot into an existing logic rather than force another top-level exception later.

06 What I'd carry forward

The hardest design decision was a scheduling decision.

The IA itself was tractable once the data was in. The real work was holding the line on sequence — convincing the org that structure had to precede features, and being specific enough about the risk that "later" didn't quietly become "never." If I ran it again, I'd instrument the old navigation earlier, so the case for restructuring was undeniable before the roadmap pressure arrived, not argued against it in the room.
