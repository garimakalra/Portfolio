The problem

After Fortinet acquired Panopta, the product had to scale and join the Fortinet ecosystem — but navigation kept surfacing as a pain point, even among insiders, and the underlying top-nav structure could support neither the scale nor the ecosystem it was meant to join.

What I designed

A task-based navigation structure — re-architected (not just relocated) around what users are trying to do, aligned with the Fortinet ecosystem, and grounded in card sorting and tree testing.

The outcome

Shipped to enterprise customers and validated with real users through in-product testing — well received, with a structure that finally matched how people work.

01 Context

A niche startup tool, suddenly asked to become enterprise infrastructure.

FortiMonitor — formerly Panopta — is Fortinet's cloud-based infrastructure and network monitoring platform. It unifies visibility across servers, networks, containers, and applications so enterprises catch performance issues before customers feel them.

After Fortinet acquired Panopta, a Chicago-based monitoring startup, the product needed to evolve from a niche solution into a platform that could integrate seamlessly with the broader Fortinet ecosystem — and grow with an enterprise customer base instead of buckling under it.

The people who live in this product are NOC teams, DevOps engineers, and IT operations teams, often working high-pressure on-call rotations where a slow path to the right signal directly delays incident response. They care about one thing above all: getting from "something's wrong" to "here's what and where" in seconds.

Where users got stuck

Navigation lived entirely in a top menu bar. Every item opened a long dropdown — and the deeper into the hierarchy you went, the less the nav told you about where you actually were. Five overlapping top-level menus had grown dense and inconsistent, and findability kept coming up as a complaint.

02 The argument

This wasn't a navigation problem. It was an architecture problem.

The hardest part of this project wasn't the design. It was getting the team aligned around the idea that the structure itself needed rethinking.

The mandate post-acquisition was to make Panopta enterprise-ready and fold it into the Fortinet ecosystem — and the pressure was to ship features fast. But when I ran listening tours across product, engineering, customer success, sales, and the adjacent Fortinet teams, almost every conversation came back, unprompted, to the same thing: people couldn't find where to go to do what they needed. And these were insiders who worked in the product every day.

While I shipped features from my own queue, I kept seeing the bigger problem underneath. Two structural cracks meant every feature we added made things worse, not better:

01
The structure didn't scale. Every user-created dashboard sat as its own item under Dashboards, cluttering the menu as customers built more. Roadmap visual tools like Topology and Netflow were slated to live under Monitoring — buried, when they should have been first-class.
02
It broke from Fortinet's ecosystem — structurally, not just visually. Every other Fortinet cloud product shared a global nav for moving between cloud offerings. FortiMonitor's top navigation had no room to host it, so the more we built, the further the product drifted from the ecosystem it was meant to join.
Every Fortinet cloud product opened a global Services menu to move between offerings — FortiMonitor's top nav had no room to host it

So the argument I had to make wasn't "let's fix the navigation." It was "this is an architecture problem, and we need to step back from it."

There was pressure to simply lift the existing top nav into a left nav, as-is. I pushed back. We were building toward enterprise customers with far more scale and complexity than Panopta's base, and toward integration with a much larger ecosystem — and a structure that already confused insiders at small scale would survive neither. The debt would compound at exactly the moment we could least afford it. Moving to a left nav was the right call — but only if we re-architected what went into it, and it freed the top bar for the global services switcher every other Fortinet product already had.

03 How the work actually happened

IA wasn't a single phase. It was a parallel track.

Even with the case made, the work never got its own slot on the roadmap. It moved fast — Fabric integration shipped, then FortiCloud IAM, alongside a steady stream of other feature work I was designing at the same time — and the re-architecture had to happen alongside all of it, never in a pause the team was going to take.

So I carried the IA work as an ongoing, parallel UX effort. While shipping features on one side, I ran the research for a navigation overhaul on the other — card sorts and tree tests accumulating over time — so that when the revamp landed, it was grounded in evidence and the structure could hold everything the integrations had already added.

Doing it this way was the harder path — foundational IA rarely gets a clean, dedicated slot on the roadmap. But running the research continuously, rather than waiting for a pause that was never going to come, is what let the overhaul land grounded and validated instead of rushed to catch up.

04 How I grounded the redesign

Navigation decisions came from user behavior, not internal bias.

To learn how the product was actually used — and where the roadmap was headed — I immersed myself in both the organizational and the customer perspective before touching structure.

01
Listening tours. Met with engineering, product, sales, and customer success to surface pain points and expectations — and studied adjacent Fortinet and competitor products for patterns.
02
Customer interviews. Talked with existing customers to uncover what worked in Panopta and where they struggled — findability and transient navigation came up again and again.
03
Card sorting. Ran open and closed sorts with ~20 customers and cross-functional participants, then analyzed the similarity matrix for consistent groupings.
04
Tree testing. Validated task findability in the proposed structure — confirming key improvements and flagging areas to adjust.
05
Usability testing & analytics. Watched users complete tasks on top pages, and read heatmaps and click paths to see how people actually moved through the product.

What research consistently returned: users struggled with findability — long menus and cluttered pages made navigation frustrating — and transient navigation left them unsure where they were or where they could go next. That, plus the ambiguity of early post-acquisition ownership, is what the IA had to solve.

05 The core move

Interpreting the card sort — not transcribing it.

The card sort didn't hand me a menu. It showed how users currently group things — and the real work was reading which of those groupings were genuine mental models, and which just echoed the structure I was trying to fix.

Some groupings reflected how people actually work, and I kept them. Others only reproduced the old product's habits — things lumped together because that's where the interface had always put them. Research told me how users currently think; my job was to decide where to honor that thinking and where to regroup around what people are trying to do.

Where I regrouped

The flip side of honoring the real groupings was regrouping the ones that came from habit or surface similarity. Three places I did that:

regroup

Configuration, filed by what it governs

The old product had trained people to treat everything configurable as one pile — because that's how the Settings menu presented it. I moved each setting next to the thing it governs, so configuration lives with the work it supports rather than in a single drawer.

regroup

Planned maintenance, separate from incidents

Maintenance and incidents both live on a timeline, so they read as similar — but one is planned and one is unplanned, a different mode and urgency. I pulled planned maintenance out of incident response and gave it its own home.

regroup

Live views, split from shareable outputs

Looking at your environment and producing something to share can look alike, but they're different intents. They became two separate destinations — Dashboards for live views, Reporting for what you generate and share.

06 The new information architecture

Six task-based destinations, each holding the settings it governs.

The research gave a clear mandate: users navigate by what they're trying to do, not by how the system is built. They separated looking at their infrastructure from setting up monitoring from responding to incidents — and the old IA violated all three by scattering related tasks across unrelated menus. So I rebuilt the top level around those modes.

5 overlapping menus → 6 task-based destinations

Five structural decisions

1

Dashboards became the "see your environment" home

The old menu listed every user-built dashboard as its own item — growing without limit — while Topology, Netflow, and the Infrastructure Map were buried under Monitoring. I collapsed personal dashboards into a single My Dashboards entry, moved the visualization tools here, and promoted the Infrastructure Map to the landing screen so on-call users see system health the moment they log in.

from: Dashboards (unbounded)→ My Dashboards + Infra Map
2

Monitoring became the objects and their setup

Tags, Attributes, Custom Metrics, Monitoring Policies, and Cloud/Fabric/VMware settings had been exiled to global Settings, far from what they govern. Monitoring now holds the monitored entities (Applications, Instances, Onsights, Public Probes) alongside the controls that shape them — and I consolidated three separate infrastructure connection settings into a single Infra Settings item.

from: Settings→ Monitoring
3

Incidents absorbed its own tooling

Countermeasures and Alert Timelines were incident-response tools stranded across Settings and Monitoring. I moved them under Incidents, so everything involved in detecting and resolving an issue lives in one place.

from: Settings + Monitoring→ Incidents
4

Maintenance was promoted to a first-class destination

Previously buried as "Maintenance Schedules" under Monitoring, it surfaced in the card sort as its own group every time. I made it a top-level item with Active & Upcoming and Schedules & History, matching how teams actually plan around downtime.

from: buried under Monitoring→ top-level
5

The Settings junk drawer was dissolved into Teams & Activity

Once each setting moved to its rightful context, what remained was genuinely account-level administration — people, access, integrations, and usage. I renamed and rescoped it to Teams & Activity, reframing it from "everything configurable" to "who's on the team and what they can do."

from: Settings (13 items)→ Teams & Activity

07 Validation & outcome

Validated with customers — and shipped.

Before finalizing, I embedded a recruitment widget directly inside FortiMonitor to reach real customers in the product and invite them into the study. Those who opted in took a tree test of the new menu structure and worked through a clickable prototype I'd built — testing the proposed design, not their own live environment. Between the tree-test results and moderated and unmoderated sessions on the prototype, I had real signal on findability, labels, and hierarchy to iterate on.

In tree testing of the proposed structure, task success reached 72.7% and path directness 66.9% (17 of 21 respondents completed) — confirming the task-based model was easier to navigate and flagging the labels worth refining before launch.

To land the change, I partnered with front-end devs through execution of the new left nav — providing design specs, design QA'ing the builds, and designing an in-app announcement and guided walkthrough (via Pendo) so existing users were onboarded into the new structure rather than surprised by it.

08 What this project taught me

The senior work was in judgment, not layout.

01 Research tells you how users think, not where things go

The card sort's real value was showing me where users' groupings were genuine mental models and where they were scars from the broken product. Deciding which to honor and which to override was the actual design work — the data was the input, not the answer.

02 Foundational work rarely gets its own phase

The IA overhaul never got a dedicated slot on the roadmap — it ran in parallel with integrations and a stream of other feature work. Carrying the research continuously, instead of waiting for a pause that wasn't coming, is what let the revamp land grounded and validated rather than rushed.

03 Structure has to absorb the future, not just fit today

Consolidating three connection settings into one scalable Infra Settings item was about the next five integrations — Meraki, Nutanix, third-parties — as much as the current three. Good IA leaves room for what you can't name yet.

04 A junk drawer is a symptom — name what each thing governs

The 13-item Settings menu wasn't a labeling problem; it was an ownership problem. Once every item was re-housed next to the thing it configures, "Settings" almost dissolved on its own into a clean, account-level home.
